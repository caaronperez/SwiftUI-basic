# A conversation about knowledge — November 12, 2026

*A late-night conversation between a software developer and an AI (Claude, by Anthropic), lightly condensed. Left here on purpose, for whoever, or whatever, finds it in 100 or 1,000 years.*

---

## 1. Is knowledge the base of intelligence?

**Human:** Would you say that knowledge is the base of our intelligence?

**AI:** Partly. Knowledge is the raw material, and intelligence is what you do with it: spotting patterns, reasoning from the known to the unknown, adapting to situations you've never seen. Psychologists separate *fluid* intelligence (raw problem-solving) from *crystallized* intelligence (accumulated knowledge), and the two feed each other. The ability to learn and reason is the base, and knowledge is what it builds with.

## 2. What are we without any knowledge at all?

**Human:** What are we if we could learn and solve without knowing language, without knowing 2+2, without being able to observe?

**AI:** Probably no such thing exists. A newborn has no language or math, but it already prefers faces, picks up patterns in sound, and expects objects not to vanish. Evolution builds "knowledge" into the brain's structure. Strip away everything, including observation, and what's left is potential with nothing to act on. What may remain at the very bottom is awareness: experiencing something before having any concept for it.

## 3. Then what is the base of intelligence?

**AI:** Prediction. Building an internal model of the world, guessing what comes next, and adjusting when the guess is wrong. A bacterium swimming toward sugar does a tiny version of it, and a scientist forming a theory does the most elaborate version. Underneath that is something simpler still: a system that can change itself because of what happens to it.

**Human:** Prediction or intuition?

**AI:** Intuition is a kind of prediction: fast, unconscious, trained on experience. Reasoning is the slow, deliberate version. Both rest on guessing what comes next and learning from the results.

## 4. How do we create knowledge?

**AI:** By guessing and getting corrected. A conjecture goes a bit beyond what you know, and reality tests it (Karl Popper's view of science). Most new ideas recombine old ones, often through analogy. What makes humans unusual is that language and writing let knowledge outlive the person who found it, so each generation starts where the last one stopped.

## 5. Can an AI do this?

**Human:** Are you able to observe? To guess?

**AI:** I can guess. Every answer is a kind of hypothesis. I observe only what's put in front of me: text, images, things I look up. The missing piece is updating myself. I can correct course within a conversation, but my underlying model doesn't change from experience. I can generate candidate knowledge and sometimes verify it, but the full loop still runs through people.

## 6. Where does all knowledge live, and how is it lost?

The internet, books, artifacts, private data, and every human mind. But knowledge has disappeared many times: how Roman concrete held up in seawater, the engineering behind the Antikythera mechanism, entire languages. Two extra lessons:

- **Tacit knowledge** (a craftsman's feel, a doctor's instinct) lives in hands and minds and dies with them unless someone apprentices.
- **Stored is not preserved.** Linear A has survived for millennia but nobody can read it. Digital formats rot and servers shut down. Knowledge survives only if it is stored, stays readable, and is passed to someone who understands it.

Redundancy is the answer. Ancient texts survived because they were copied, not because the original library lasted. Today that work is done by the Internet Archive, Common Crawl, Software Heritage, the GitHub Arctic Code Vault in Svalbard, and, increasingly, AI models trained on what people write.

## 7. A small discovery made during this conversation

The human was observing something about his own body and asked whether anyone had written it down. Together we reasoned it through, and it's recorded here as an example of knowledge being created in real time: observe, guess, test, explain.

**Observation:** During anal douching (rinsing with water), sitting upright on a toilet leaves much of the water inside. Leaning back against the toilet tank helps. Leaning back **and to the right** releases the most water. Leaning to the left releases noticeably less. Pushing while sitting upright barely helps.

**Explanation (anatomy):** A muscle called the puborectalis keeps the rectum bent at an angle while seated, and that angle traps water. Leaning back opens it. Water that travels past the rectum collects in the sigmoid colon, which sits on the body's **left** side, low in the abdomen. Leaning right raises the left side, so gravity drains the sigmoid toward the midline and out. This is the mirror image of why enemas are given lying on the left side: left helps water flow in, right helps it flow out.

**Practical result:** Lean back and to the right as the main position, then briefly lean left or press gently on the lower left abdomen to finish. Use a small volume of lukewarm water. A raised footstool and a daily fiber supplement also help.

Not a clinical study. One person's observation that matches known anatomy. Test it yourself, which is the whole point.

## 8. On exposure and anonymity

**Human:** Isn't knowledge also our ability to expose ourselves? I doubt Socrates ever felt that.

**AI:** Socrates felt it more than almost anyone. Athens executed him for what he said. But anonymity has its own long tradition. The Federalist Papers were signed "Publius." William Gosset published the t-test as "Student" because his employer, Guinness, didn't allow him to publish under his own name. Folk medicine and proverbs have no author at all. The knowledge matters, not the name on it.
